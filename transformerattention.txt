What your method does, in simple words

Your method creates a heatmap showing which image patches received the strongest attention in the final Vision Transformer layer.

1. The image is divided into patches

The Vision Transformer does not examine the image as individual pixels. It divides the image into small squares.

For a 224 × 224 image using 16 × 16 patches, it creates:

14 × 14 = 196 image patches

Each patch becomes a token.

2. The transformer compares every patch with every other patch

The model contains several attention heads. Each head may notice different relationships.

For example:

one head may focus on edges;
another may focus on a face;
another may focus on background patterns;
another may focus on textures.

The attention matrix records how strongly each patch attends to every other patch.

3. You use only the final transformer layer

Your code selects:

attentions[-1]

This is the final attention layer. It contains the most processed attention relationships produced by the vision encoder.

You are not combining attention from all layers. That is why your method is called:

Max-Fusion Terminal-Layer Attention Projection

and not standard Attention Rollout.

4. Max-Fusion keeps the strongest attention head

For every relationship between two patches, you compare the values from all attention heads and retain the largest one.

In simple terms:

If any attention head strongly notices a relationship, preserve it.

This can create sharper heatmaps than averaging all heads.

However, it can also preserve one noisy or biased attention head, which may explain the repeated right-edge activation.

5. Max Projection gives one score to every patch

After Max-Fusion, you still have a large matrix showing relationships between every pair of patches.

You then ask:

What is the strongest attention this patch received from any other patch?

That gives one attention score for each of the 196 patches.

For example:

Patch 1: 0.20
Patch 2: 0.84
Patch 3: 0.35

Patch 2 becomes more strongly highlighted.

6. The scores are reshaped into the image grid

The 196 scores are reconstructed into a 14 × 14 layout matching the original patch positions.

This gives a small spatial attention map.

7. The map is normalised

The weakest patch becomes close to 0, and the strongest becomes 1.

This means the colours are relative to that particular image:

red/orange: strongest attention;
yellow/green: medium attention;
blue: weakest attention.

Red does not automatically mean “AI artifact.” It only means “strongest in this heatmap.”

8. The map is enlarged and placed over the image

The 14 × 14 map is resized to 224 × 224, coloured and blended with the image.

The result shows where the model had its strongest final-layer attention relationships.

What the heatmap actually means

The correct interpretation is:

The highlighted regions received strong attention in the final Vision Transformer layer and may have influenced the model.

It does not prove:

why the model predicted AI-generated or real;
that the highlighted region contains an AI artifact;
that the region caused the prediction;
that unhighlighted regions were unimportant.
What perturbation testing does

Perturbation testing checks whether the highlighted region actually matters to the classifier.

Step 1: Run the original image

Suppose the detector gives:

AI-generated probability: 91%
Step 2: Select the strongest heatmap region

You take the top highlighted area, such as the strongest 20% of attention values.

Step 3: Blur that region

Instead of adding a black box, you blur the highlighted region.

Blurring removes some visual information while avoiding an unnatural black area.

Step 4: Run the modified image again

The detector processes the blurred image.

Suppose the result becomes:

AI-generated probability: 63%
Step 5: Measure the confidence drop
Original confidence: 91%
Blurred confidence: 63%
Confidence drop: 28%

A large drop suggests the highlighted area contained information that supported the original prediction.

A small drop suggests the highlighted area may not have been very important.

A negative drop means the model became more confident after the region was blurred, which weakens the heatmap’s faithfulness.

Why compare with a random region?

Blurring any large part of an image may change the prediction. Therefore, you should also blur a random region of the same size.

Example:

Highlighted-region drop: 28%
Random-region drop:       5%

This supports the explanation because the highlighted region affected the model much more than a random region.

But:

Highlighted-region drop: 6%
Random-region drop:      7%

This suggests the heatmap region may not be more meaningful than chance.

For the recurring right edge

Run three equal-sized tests:

Blur the highlighted right-edge region.
Blur the strongest non-edge hotspot.
Blur a random region.

Then compare the confidence drops.

This tells you whether the right edge is:

genuinely influencing the prediction;
merely a positional attention habit;
or an artifact produced by Max-Fusion.
Whole method in one sentence

Your method finds the strongest final-layer attention relationships between image patches, converts them into a heatmap, and then tests the highlighted regions by blurring them and checking whether the classifier’s confidence decreases.
